Inclusive Learning Design in Certificate III Hairdressing Introduction Inclusive learning design in adult VET hairdressing training is part of teaching technical skills, not separate from it. This becomes clear when learners shift from watching a demonstration to cutting hair themselves with control. At Box Hill Institute in Certificate III Hairdressing, I see this issue starting before SHBHCUT006 Combine haircut structures. It begins in SHBHCUT001 Design haircut structures, SHBHCUT002 solid form/one length, SHBHCUT003 graduated haircut, and SHBHCUT004 increased layer and uniform layer. By SHBHCUT006, learners must combine these haircut structures, and the gap between understanding and doing grows. The challenge is more than remembering haircut terms. Learners must link the seven cutting procedures — head position, parting, sectioning, distribution, projection, finger/shear position, and design line with mobile and stationary guides — into one process. Many can name these steps in class, but naming them does not always turn into correct action on mannequins or client models. The guide can still disappear in their hands. Learners require repeated and clear ways to shift from copying blindly to practising with intention. Competency should not depend on chance. When learners start to get lost The challenge in SHBHCUT006 does not only appear at the final combine stage. It often begins when learners shift from solid form to graduation. Solid form uses 0degree projection and natural distribution, which gives learners a steady base to practise parting, sectioning, and controlling design lines. Graduation tends to be the first stage where I notice many learners losing control of the haircut. Before this, learners generally feel confident. Introducing diagonal parting and projection makes that confidence less steady. In the salon classroom, errors become clear fast. Partings lose definition. One side may carry more weight. Projection angles fall without the learner realising it. Sometimes the guide disappears midway through a section. Learners might continue cutting but cannot pinpoint if the issue started with parting, projection, or pulling too much hair into the next subsection. At this point, learners face one combined decision involving all seven cutting procedures. Cognitive load theory helps explain this difficulty. The Centre for Education Statistics and Evaluation (CESE, 2017) states that working memory has limits when learners handle new or complex information. Chandler and Sweller (1991) also show that instructional format can increase or reduce unnecessary cognitive load. In haircutting, overload is not just mental; comb tension, elbow height, section size, scissor control, and guide awareness all compete for the learner’s focus. I do not see this as laziness. Many learners are trying before these actions become automatic. CESE (2017) and Chandler and Sweller (1991) explain why learners get overloaded, but they do not fully show how learners gain practical control. Billett (2013) offers another view. He discusses modelling, coaching, and scaffolding, noting that some practice-based knowledge cannot come from discovery alone (pp. 149–150). Kirschner et al. (2006) also argue that novice learners need clear guidance instead of being left to figure out complex procedures by themselves. In haircutting, learners cannot master projection just by watching a professional demonstration. They must practise, receive corrections, and repeat the skill. Showing the haircut clearly A demonstration reveals the educator’s actions, but it often leaves the educator’s thinking hidden. In graduation, I avoid sending learners directly from watching to full practice. Instead, I start with the Design Decision Chart to outline the triangular form, then describe how projection and a stationary guide influence the weight line. I also show low, medium, and high projection angles on the mannequin so learners can observe how slight angle shifts change the weight placement. Some learners mimic the hand movements but still do not grasp why the weight forms there. Universal Design for Learning helps address the overload described by cognitive load theory. When learners cannot keep all steps in working memory, tools like diagrams, mannequin comparisons, and slower section-by-section practice offer alternative ways to approach the haircut. CAST (2024) defines UDL as a framework to improve teaching and learning. Jwad et al. (2022) note that learner diversity includes physical, sensory, attention, communication, and other access challenges. In class, learners work with shape diagrams, design decision charts, mannequin comparisons, and before-and-after samples. They do more than listen; they search for the guide, verify the weight line, and match the cut to what they see. Some learners still need the task broken down further. Learners with ADHD, ASD, mental health issues, or less confidence might focus on one step at a time. They might practise diagonal parting before adding projection or repeat a section until the guideline is clear. While they practise, I may adjust elbow, wrist, or finger angles to stop errors from repeating throughout the haircut. Scaffolding offers temporary help toward deliberate cutting. Reasonable adjustment and classroom reality Inclusive learning design also needs to be proactive. In practical haircutting classes, some learners do not ask for help directly. A few learners stay quiet, repeatedly say “I don’t know”, or wait for the educator before they continue cutting. Others become frustrated when classmates move faster, and some lose confidence after one cutting mistake. I do not read this as lack of effort straight away. Before moving on, many learners need a clear sequence to follow in practical classes. In these moments, I usually ask: are they stuck, done, tired, or quietly waiting for a short pause? When learners pause, they may need a short moment to process what they have just practised. I demonstrate beside their work and compare my section to theirs, then ask questions to help them see what differs if they continue. I want learners to build judgement, not only wait for my answer. This connects with reasonable adjustment. The Australian Government Department of Employment and Workplace Relations (DEWR, 2026) explains that reasonable adjustments support access and participation while considering unit requirements and assessment integrity. Reasonable adjustment should not be misunderstood as lowering the standard. For SHBHCUT006, it gives learners a fairer pathway toward competency, but the haircut still has to meet the required technical outcome. This creates an important tension: support must move learners toward independence, not dependence on the teacher. Classroom limits remain. When one educator works with many learners, inclusive learning design in hairdressing becomes hard. Teaching technical skills through whole-class demonstration falls short because the educator must watch body position, hand control, elbow angle, sectioning, and projection closely. Uneven haircuts can result from different issues: one learner might lose the guide due to a thick subsection, while another might get a similar uneven cut because their elbow drops during projection. The outside result looks alike, but the fix is not the same. Billett’s guided practice idea helps, yet haircutting classrooms reveal its limits. One-to-one correction matters since the educator spots small technical details learners often miss, like a dropped elbow, loose tension, a guide pulled from the wrong place, or a thick subsection. Time is the challenge. While one learner gets correction, others wait, holding scissors but unsure about their next cut. This waiting can lower motivation, confidence, practice quality, and assessment. Tools and peer checks offer some support but cannot replace direct feedback. Maintaining standards while improving access Inclusive learning design does not lower the haircutting standard. Hairdressing outcomes still must meet commercial expectations, focusing on shape control, length control, and symmetry.
